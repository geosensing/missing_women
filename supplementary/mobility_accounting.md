# Supplementary Information: accounting for the street gap

The headline compares the observed pedestrian sex ratio to the residential sex ratio and reports
the difference as women missing from the street. **That number is not adjusted here.** The mobility
gaps in the Time Use Survey are not an explanation of the street gap but the same exclusion recorded
one step upstream, at the front door instead of on the road; women kept at home are the largest
component of the absence, not a confound to remove. The purpose of this section is to locate the
gap: how much of it opens at the front door, how much in trip counts, and how much remains that
only street imagery can see.

## What the Time Use Survey says

Goel (2023) analyses the 2019 Time Use Survey for urban residents of India and reports two
quantities by sex. The mobility rate is the share of individuals who made at least one trip on the
reporting day. The trip rate is trips per day over all respondents, so it already embeds the
mobility rate.

| | Women | Men | Female / male |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Mobility rate (made at least one trip) | 47.3% | 86% | 0.550 |
| Trip rate (trips per day, all respondents) | 1.32 | 2.93 | 0.451 |
| Trips per *mobile* person (implied) | 2.79 | 3.41 | 0.819 |

The third row is implied by the first two rather than reported, and it is the smaller effect. Almost
all of the gender difference in mobility is the decision to leave the house at all; conditional on
leaving, women make about 18% fewer trips.

These are not marginal facts about transport. In the 2011--12 IHDS-II, 78% of urban women reported
needing permission to visit a grocery store, 89% to visit a relative or friend in the neighbourhood,
and 94% to travel a short distance by bus or train.

## The accounting

Scaling each city's residential sex ratio by each factor in turn partitions the gap. All quantities
are women per 1,000 men, so the parts add exactly:

```
residential − observed
    = [residential − residential×0.550]   women not out of the home
    + [residential×0.550 − residential×0.451]   women out, but making fewer trips
    + [residential×0.451 − observed]      unexplained by published mobility
```

## What the residual is, and what it is not

In Mumbai, Navi Mumbai and Delhi, roughly a quarter of the street gap survives everything a
time-use survey can measure. These are women who left home, made their trips, and are still not
among pedestrian sightings.

**This is the quantity the instrument uniquely provides.** A time-use survey records who left the
house; it cannot record who is standing on a road. The residual is where the two kinds of evidence
stop overlapping.

It is a residual, not a mechanism. It is consistent with several things the design cannot separate:
women's trips being shorter, or more often enclosed in vehicles, or routed away from the sampled
road classes, or concentrated in hours the fieldwork did not cover. It is also consistent with the
reading in Phadke, Khan and Ranade (2011), whose subject is these streets: that women's presence
outdoors is conditionally permissible, tolerated when it can be justified by a destination and an
errand, while men need not account for being outside. That account predicts exactly a residual,
because it is a claim about lingering rather than about commuting — and trip counts measure
commuting.

Two of the paper's own results bear on this. Women's share among two-wheeler riders is *lower* than
among pedestrians, not higher, so private mobility does not absorb the gap. And the shortfall holds
across road classes rather than concentrating in one.

## Bangalore is the informative contrast, and its residual is fragile

Bangalore's residual is small: its street presence is very nearly what published mobility rates
would predict. That is consistent with it being the highest female-share city in the data.

It should be reported as approximately zero rather than as a small positive number. Under a ±10%
change in the residential sex ratio the residuals move as follows:

## Limitations

**The mobility rates are national urban averages applied to four specific cities.** If women in
Mumbai, Delhi and Bangalore are more mobile than the urban average — plausible, given higher incomes
and more formal employment — then the explained shares are overstated and the residuals reported
here are lower bounds. Recomputing the mobility and trip rates for Maharashtra-urban, Delhi and
Karnataka-urban from the Time Use Survey unit records would settle this, and is the single most
useful extension of this section.

**Trips are not street exposure.** A trip taken door to door by car contributes almost nothing to
the imagery; a walk contributes a great deal. Scaling by trip counts therefore treats a mode
difference as though it were an exposure difference. A person-minutes benchmark built from the
time-use activity records would be tighter.

**The residential benchmark is age 20+, not exactly the annotators' adult threshold.** The official
Census 2011 C-14 City workbook provides five-year age bands. The code sums ages 20--24 through 80+
within the municipal unit for each city and excludes age not stated. An exact age-18+ benchmark
would require an assumption about the 18- and 19-year-olds within the 15--19 band. The sensitivity
table above shows why that choice matters more for Bangalore than for the other cities.

**The decomposition is sequential, so the order matters.** Attributing the extensive margin first
and the intensive margin second assigns their shared variation to the first term. Reversing the
order would move part of the extensive-margin share into the intensive-margin share. It would not
affect the residual, which is what is claimed.

## Policy relevance

The constraint is not immovable. Rathore and Singh (2026) exploit the staggered rollout of free bus
travel for women across five Indian states, using both rounds of the Time Use Survey, and find gains
in women's paid work participation that are **larger in more patriarchal districts** — consistent
with the schemes relieving a mobility constraint rather than a budget constraint. If most of the
street gap is women not leaving home, then interventions that lower the cost of leaving home act on
the largest component of it.
